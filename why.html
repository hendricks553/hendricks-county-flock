The concern isn't our officers. It's the technology.

There is no reason to doubt that a local chief believes in this program for the right reasons, or that it has helped on real cases here. That is not what is in question. What follows is about the system these departments have bought into, and the people outside this county it hands our data to.

What the database actually is

An ALPR does not watch for a suspect. It photographs every car that passes and files the plate, the place, and the time into a database that holds the movements of vehicles in town for the retention window, historically 30 days.

Continuous movement tracking reveals what the Supreme Court has called the privacies of life. Where you worship, who you visit, which doctor you see, how often you go and how long you stay. None of it requires anyone to have suspected you of anything. The pattern is kept just in case, and the systems protecting it have more failure points than anyone accounted for when the contract was signed.

Nobody objects to being seen on a public road. Arguing that point wastes the three minutes you get at a microphone. The objection is to the record that accumulates, and to who can reach it.

Failure point one: the machine makes mistakes

Nobody watches these cameras around the clock. A program does, and it gets things wrong.

Two widely reported cases involved adults ordered out of their car at gunpoint in front of children after a camera misread a plate. The Institute for Justice has documented roughly 30 stops caused by misread plates, most of them with officers drawing firearms. A single character read wrong is enough.

Even when the plate is read correctly, the list it is checked against can be stale. This is the failure people miss, and it is the one with the hardest evidence behind it.

Los Angeles counted, and the answer was one in three.

An LAPD Inspector General audit reviewed 498 stolen-vehicle alerts over two months. 337 correctly identified a stolen car. 161 did not, a false-positive rate of 32.3%. Each of those 161 meant an officer approaching a driver who had stolen nothing, often as a high-risk stop. The department let its Flock contract expire in July 2026.

The cause is worth being precise about, because it is not a story about bad cops or bad cameras. The plates were largely read correctly. The hotlists were wrong: recovered vehicles never cleared from the list, outdated theft reports, data entry errors nobody corrected. It is a records-maintenance failure, and it is completely invisible until somebody audits it.

That leaves officers filtering alerts by hand or risking a stop on an innocent resident. It is a burden placed on them by the system, not a failing of theirs. And no agency in this county publishes a comparable figure, because nobody outside those departments has counted.

Failure point two: private from us, open to them

Use policies correctly treat this data as sensitive and keep it out of public review. Meanwhile it flows into a network spanning the country. Plainfield's portal, the only local one that publishes its sharing lists, shows agencies in more than 30 states with access, along with federal entities.

So the data is private from the residents it describes, and available to thousands of officers elsewhere who are a login and a brief stated reason away from it. Anything they pull, they can keep, in a copy nobody here controls.

The Institute for Justice has documented roughly 30 cases of officers using their access to stalk people. What matters as much as the count is how they surfaced: most came to light because a victim came forward, not because an internal control caught it.

This is not a distant problem.

In August 2026 an IMPD officer was found to have used the Flock system hundreds of times for no legitimate law enforcement purpose. He was stripped of police powers on August 6, his system access revoked, and Chief Tanya Terry suspended him with a recommendation for termination. Administrative and criminal investigations followed, and IMPD moved to require every search be tied to a case number.

The department's handling of it was fast and public, and that deserves saying. But the reporting indicates the searches came to light around the time a newspaper contacted the department, not through routine auditing. That is the pattern nationally too, and it is the argument for oversight rather than against any particular department.

Failure point three: the vendor's own security

Even granting every agency in the chain acts properly, the data still sits with a company whose security record is documented and poor.

  • In late 2025, researchers found Flock cameras streaming live to the open internet with no username, no password, and no encryption. 404 Media confirmed at least 60; researcher Jon Gaines catalogued 67 exposed feeds and debug interfaces. Archived footage could be downloaded and device settings changed.
  • Gaines published 51 findings across Flock's device ecosystem, including its plate reader cameras and gunshot detection microphones. MITRE assigned CVEs to 22 of them, with more pending.
  • Flock login credentials have appeared for sale on Russian cybercrime forums, and a cybersecurity firm reported at least 35 customer accounts with stolen passwords. Multi-factor authentication was not required of law enforcement customers until the company's August 2026 changes.
  • Senator Ron Wyden and Representative Raja Krishnamoorthi wrote to the FTC in November 2025 asking for an investigation into these issues.

A company that assures cities its deployed devices are secure, while dozens of them sit open to anyone who knows where to look, is asking for a level of trust its record does not support.

And the promised results are hard to find

Atlanta is the most camera-saturated city in the country and an early adopter, with more than 5,000 Flock cameras. It is Flock's hometown. If this technology delivers what is claimed, it should be visible there.

The Atlanta Community Press Collective pulled the police department's own figures from the FBI's Crime Data Explorer. Between 2021 and 2025, as the camera network grew several times over, clearance rates fell for homicide, robbery, larceny, and shoplifting. Homicide clearance went from 53.4% to 48%, the department's worst year of the period. Rape clearance was flat. Motor vehicle theft, the category these cameras should be best at, moved by about a third of a percentage point and sits near one in ten.

Falling clearance rates are not Flock's fault, and nobody should claim they are. Departments were dealing with staffing, a national auto theft surge, and much else. But that is the point. If a city can grow to 5,000 cameras and its own reported numbers show no lift, that undercuts the claim that this degree of surveillance should be accepted for the greater good.

The question isn't whether you trust your department

If the only people to trust here were the local police department, there would be no reason to raise any of this.

But the system asks for trust in agencies across the country operating under their own rules, in a company with a documented history of security failures, and, given credentials for sale on criminal forums, in strangers. With the privacies of life of everyone who drives through town.

What to ask for

  1. End the contract. Bloomington let its Flock contract lapse in 2026 after a months-long evaluation, and reporting this year puts cancellations or rejections across roughly 28 states. The county functioned before these cameras and would function after.
  2. If that can't happen quickly, three concrete limits. Formal limits on who may search the database and on what basis. Sharing restricted to our own department. Renewals brought to a public agenda with enough time before the notice deadline that the council is actually making a choice rather than letting one pass.
  3. And answer the settings question. Flock changed several defaults in August 2026. What is the retention window here today, are searches tied to case numbers, who reviews the audit logs, and where can a resident read the answer? None of that is published for any agency in this county, and none of it is hard to answer.

Policy and public opinion are moving in this direction. Indiana lawmakers have signaled they will take up plate reader regulation, and the local police union in Indianapolis has itself called for additional safeguards from the legislature. There is an argument for being ahead of that rather than behind it.

The four objections, and fair answers

"Your phone already tracks you."

Fair as far as it goes. The difference is consent and reversibility. You can leave a phone at home or turn location off. The state requires you to display a license plate.

"It solved a case."

Those cases are real and worth acknowledging. They answer a different question. Nothing in the ask stops a department using the system on a case. The ask is that somebody outside it can check how it was used.

"There's nothing to hide in public."

The misuse record is the reply. The people tracked in those cases were not hiding anything either. What made them reachable was that a location history existed and somebody with access wanted it.

"The data is never sold."

Probably true, and beside the point. No money changes hands in any of the sharing documented on this site. One query reaches cameras across the country under the querying agency's rules. Selling is not the mechanism.
