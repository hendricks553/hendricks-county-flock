It is not the camera. It is the database.

Nobody has a right not to be seen on a public road. That is not the objection, and arguing it wastes the three minutes you get at a microphone. Here is the version that holds up.

The four points

  1. A photograph is not the product. A searchable history is. One camera seeing your car is nothing. A hundred and eight of them logging plate, place, and time into a system any participating agency across thirty-odd states can query, without a warrant, under that agency's rules rather than ours, is a different thing. What accumulates is a location history for everyone who drives here, including the overwhelming majority who will never be suspected of anything.
  2. Misuse is documented, not hypothetical. In August 2026 the Washington Post reported at least 50 cases nationally of officers charged with or accused of using plate reader systems to surveil women they knew personally, with Flock's system involved in 46 of them. These are individual bad actors out of many thousands of users, which is exactly why the relevant question is not whether local officers are trustworthy but whether the system catches it when someone is not. Most of these cases surfaced from outside scrutiny rather than a department catching itself.
  3. Indiana sets no rules, so every safeguard is voluntary. No state law governs retention, sharing, or oversight of this data. The only ALPR bill in the General Assembly, in 2015, died in committee without a vote. That leaves each department to write its own rules, which is a burden as much as a freedom. Most departments here have not been asked to.
  4. The ask is modest and other towns have already granted it. End the contract, or adopt a policy the public can read and somebody outside the department can audit. That is not an abolitionist position, and several communities have landed on the second option rather than the first. In August 2026 Flock itself conceded a number of the technical pieces.

The August 2026 concessions and the hole in them

After months of coverage, Flock announced changes on August 13, 2026: default retention dropped from 30 days to 7, case codes required for searches, automatic lockouts when search behavior looks abnormal, audit review required of customers by the end of 2026, multi-factor authentication, and a channel for security researchers to report vulnerabilities.

Those are real improvements and worth saying so. They are also, every one of them, either a default a customer can change or an obligation a customer enforces on itself. A shorter default retention window is not a rule about retention. A required audit is not an audited department unless somebody outside it reads the audit.

Which makes for a fair local question.

Ask your council or your department: which of those settings has been adopted, what is the retention window today, who reviews the search logs, and where can a resident read that answer? Nobody in this county has published one yet. It costs nothing to answer, and the answer is either reassuring or useful.

Read the hit rates carefully

The three local portals that publish numbers report hit rates between roughly 7% and 22%. That is between one car in fourteen and one car in four and a half being flagged.

A hit is not a crime. It means a plate matched a list, and lists carry stolen vehicles alongside expired registrations, unrelated warrants, and entries other agencies added for their own reasons. The published numbers do not say how many hits were wrong. They do not say how many produced a stop.

Both readings are worth raising. If the rate reflects genuine matches, a striking share of ordinary traffic is being flagged. If it does not, the flagging is noisier than a system used to justify stops should be. Nobody outside the departments can tell which, because the follow-through is not published.

An hour away, this already worked

Bloomington ended its Flock contract in March 2026.

After months of public pressure the city let its roughly $50,000 contract expire on March 5 and declined to renew. Before that it had already barred out-of-state agencies from querying its database. Afterward it cut outside sharing entirely.

The framing worth borrowing is the mayor's own: if a tool like this is used at all, it should be under narrow parameters, real accountability, and safeguards the public can see.

Bloomington is not alone, and the list grew through 2026. The ACLU's Get the Flock Out campaign tracks councils that have cancelled contracts or declined renewals, including several votes in August 2026. A council is more receptive to a decision other councils have already made.

The four objections, and fair answers

"Your phone already tracks you."

True, and a fair point as far as it goes. The difference is consent and reversibility: you can leave a phone at home or turn location off. You cannot leave a license plate at home, because the state requires you to display it.

"It solved a case."

Those cases are real and worth acknowledging. They are also an answer to a different question. Nothing in the ask stops a department from using the system on a case. The ask is that somebody outside it can check how it was used.

"There is nothing to hide in public."

The misuse record is the reply. In the documented cases the people tracked were not hiding anything either. What made them reachable was that a location history existed and somebody with access wanted it.

"The data is never sold."

Probably true, and beside the point. No money changes hands in any of the sharing documented on this site. One query reaches cameras across the country under the querying agency's rules. Selling is not the mechanism.
