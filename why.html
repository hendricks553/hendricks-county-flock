It is not the camera. It is the database.

Nobody has a right not to be seen on a public road. That is not the objection, and arguing it wastes the three minutes you get at a microphone. Here is the argument that holds up.

The four points

  1. A photograph is not the product. A searchable history is. One camera seeing your car is nothing. A hundred and eight cameras logging plate, place, and time into a system that any participating agency in thirty-odd states can query, without a warrant, under that agency's rules rather than ours, is a different object entirely. The thing being built is a location history for everyone who drives here.
  2. Misuse is documented, not hypothetical. In August 2026 the Washington Post reported at least 50 cases of officers charged with or accused of using plate reader systems to surveil women they knew personally, with Flock's system involved in 46 of them. Cases include an officer checking whether an ex had visited an abortion clinic and a chief who used the system to track and confront a former partner. Most surfaced from outside scrutiny rather than a department catching itself.
  3. Indiana sets no rules, so every safeguard is voluntary. No state law governs retention, sharing, or oversight of this data. The only ALPR bill in the General Assembly, in 2015, died in committee without a vote. That leaves each department writing its own rules, or not writing any. In this county, three of six have not shown that they have.
  4. The ask is modest and other towns have already granted it. End the contract, or adopt a policy the public can read and somebody outside the department can audit. This is not an abolitionist position. It is the position Flock's own critics have been asking for, and in August 2026 the company conceded several of the technical pieces.

The August 2026 concessions and the hole in them

After months of coverage, Flock announced changes on August 13, 2026: default retention dropped from 30 days to 7, case codes required for searches, automatic lockouts when search behavior looks abnormal, audit review required of customers by the end of 2026, multi-factor authentication, and a channel for security researchers to report vulnerabilities.

Take the win. Then read what it is. Every item on that list is either a default a customer can change or an obligation a customer has to enforce on itself. A shorter default retention window is not a rule about retention. A required audit is not an audited department, unless somebody who does not work there reads the audit.

This is the local question, and it is a fair one.

Ask your council or your department: which of those settings have you actually adopted, what is your retention window today, who reviews the search logs, and where can a resident read that answer? No Hendricks County agency has published one. It costs nothing to answer, and the answer is either reassuring or informative.

Read the hit rates carefully

The three local portals that publish numbers report hit rates between roughly 7% and 22%. That is between one car in fourteen and one car in four and a half being flagged.

A hit is not a crime. It means a plate matched a list, and lists carry stolen vehicles alongside expired registrations, unrelated warrants, and entries other agencies added for their own reasons. The published numbers do not say how many hits were wrong. They do not say how many produced a stop.

Both directions of that ambiguity are worth raising. If the rate is real, a striking share of ordinary traffic is being flagged. If it is not, the flagging is unreliable in a system used to justify stops. Nobody outside the departments can tell which, because nobody publishes the follow-through.

An hour away, this already worked

Bloomington ended its Flock contract in March 2026.

After months of public pressure the city let its roughly $50,000 contract expire on March 5 and declined to renew. Before that it had already barred out-of-state agencies from querying its database. Afterward it cut outside sharing entirely.

The framing worth borrowing is the mayor's own: if a tool like this is used at all, it should be under narrow parameters, real accountability, and safeguards the public can see.

Bloomington is not alone, and the list grew through 2026. The ACLU's Get the Flock Out campaign tracks councils that have cancelled contracts or declined renewals, including several votes in August 2026 alone. Councils respond to the argument that other councils have already accepted it.

Answering the three deflections

"Your phone already tracks you."

True, and consented to, revocable, and not operated by the agency that can arrest you. You can leave a phone at home. You cannot leave a license plate at home, because the state requires you to display it.

"It solved a case."

Individual cases are real and nobody disputes them. They are also not an answer to a question about rules. Nothing in the ask stops the department from using the system on a case. The ask is that somebody outside the department can check how it was used.

"There is nothing to hide in public."

The misuse record is the reply. In the documented cases, the people surveilled were not hiding anything either. What made them vulnerable was that a location history about them existed and somebody with access wanted it.

"The data is never sold."

Probably true, and beside the point. No money changes hands in any of the sharing documented on this site. One query reaches cameras across the country under the querying agency's rules. Selling is not the mechanism.
